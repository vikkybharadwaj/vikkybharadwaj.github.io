The problem

Knowledge scatters faster than teams can decide.

Customer evidence, design, code reality, strategy, and analytics each live in a different place — and drift apart the moment they’re written. So the quality of a decision ends up depending on who’s in the room and what they happen to remember.

Symptom 01

Folklore decisions

“I think we decided that because of something a customer said” — but the quote, the trade-off, and the reasoning are gone.

Symptom 02

Reconstruction tax

Every decision re-opens with someone spending half a day re-assembling context that already existed, somewhere.

Symptom 03

Silent drift

Strategy changes; the roadmap, the specs, and the briefs don’t. Nobody notices until they contradict each other in a review.

Who it’s for

Built for teams that decide together — not every team, on purpose.

A system like this earns its keep in a specific situation. Naming who it’s for — and who it isn’t, yet — is what kept it from sprawling into a tool that does everything and decides nothing.

Squarely for

  • Product teams — PM, design, eng, leadership — who decide together and keep losing the thread between them
  • Orgs where customer evidence, strategy, code reality and analytics live in different tools and drift apart
  • Teams already working with an AI agent in the loop, who want it reading the same context they do
  • People willing to spend a little upkeep to stop paying the reconstruction tax every week

Deliberately not (yet)

  • Solo side-projects with no shared context to lose
  • Teams that won’t maintain it — an operating system you don’t feed becomes a docs folder again
  • Anyone wanting a passive wiki to read, not a system that acts
  • A drop-in replacement for the tracker, the design tool, or the warehouse — it sits alongside them

The honest version: this was built around one real team’s way of deciding, then generalized to the teams that share its shape. It isn’t trying to serve every org, every process, every tool — that focus is the point.

The bet

Treat the body of product knowledge as a system — with infrastructure, not just documents.

A docs folder decays. A system maintains itself. The insight: if the same context the team uses to decide is also structured for an AI agent to read, you can wrap it in lightweight infrastructure that keeps it fresh, flags its own drift, and compounds every time it’s used.

A docs folder

  • Read once, then rots
  • Context lives in people’s heads
  • Drift is invisible until it bites
  • Onboarding = asking around
  • The same questions, re-answered weekly

An operating system

  • Refreshes itself every session
  • Context is loaded, cited, and compounding
  • Drift is flagged before merge
  • Onboarding = the system explains itself
  • Answers in seconds — evidence-backed

Where it fits

“Isn’t this just a wiki?” — no, and here’s the line.

The obvious reaction to “treat knowledge as a system” is “we already have tools for that.” We do — and this doesn’t replace them. It occupies the gap they leave: the connective tissue that keeps decisions grounded while everything else drifts.

The wiki

Notion / Confluence

Great at storing what someone wrote down. But a page is read once and rots — it can’t notice when it’s gone stale or pull fresh source on its own.

The line: this maintains the knowledge instead of just holding it.

The point tool

Productboard / Linear

Excellent at their slice — feedback, or tickets. But each owns one stage, so the thread from customer evidence to shipped work still breaks at the seams.

The line: this is the connective layer across them, not another silo.

The bolt-on bot

A chatbot on your docs

Answers from whatever it’s pointed at — including the stale and the contradictory, with no sense of what should govern a decision.

The line: observe / judge / execute stay distinct, and answers are cited and governed.

The wedge in one line

Not a place to store knowledge, or a tool for one stage — the system that keeps every decision grounded in current, cited evidence.

It deliberately sits alongside the wiki, the tracker and the warehouse — reading from them, keeping them honest, and closing the loop from customer insight to execution that no single one of them owns.

The architecture · the centerpiece

Six layers that keep the picture assembled.

The design idea worth stealing: a clean separation of concerns between what observes, what judges, and what executes — with a governance layer whose only job is to stop the system from ever drifting away from its own description.

The core division of labour

Hooks — they observe

6 lifecycle scripts

Deterministic shell scripts the harness fires on session start / end / prompt. They pull fresh source, detect structural drift, and load memory. They notice and surface — they never decide.

Policies — they judge

23 decision rules

Plain-language rules loaded only when their trigger fires. “When a PR touches strategy, walk these six alignment signals.” Judgement, encoded once, applied consistently.

Skills — they execute

10 packaged workflows

Reusable multi-step procedures invoked on demand. Turn an interview into a structured brief; critique a feature; audit strategy drift. Tools, not instructions.

The full stack

The three primitives above (hooks, policies, skills) sit inside a fuller stack — bookended by the anchors that give every session its frame and the memory and governance that make it compound and stay honest.

Anchors

Every session frames itself

Two documents — the target user and the core job — that every piece of work is checked against. If a suggestion can’t trace to them, it’s drift.

Hooks

Deterministic upkeep

Fresh source every session, drift flagged automatically, cross-session memory loaded. Infrastructure, not documentation.

Policies

Situational judgement

23 rules for how to judge a situation, trigger-loaded so context stays lean. The team’s “how we decide” made explicit.

Skills

Reusable workflows

10 packaged procedures. Each use adds to a compounding pattern library instead of being re-improvised.

Compounding memory

Nothing learned is lost

A learnings log, a backlog with rationale, a cross-session episode index, a feedback loop. Structured memory, not a filing cabinet.

Governance

Drift-proof by design

The system audits itself. (See below — this is the part I’m proudest of.)

The idea I’d put on a whiteboard in an interview

A system that can’t silently drift from its own description.

Every new primitive — a hook, a policy, a skill, a folder — must update every registry that references it, including the system’s own visual self-portrait. Add a policy without registering it everywhere and the change fails its own audit: a structural-drift check flags it at session start, and a review gate blocks the merge. The map can never quietly fall out of sync with the territory. That’s the difference between a project that decays the moment its author leaves and one that explains itself to the next person.

Why three kinds of primitive

Hooks vs. policies vs. skills.

Most “AI workflow” setups blur these together. Keeping them distinct is what makes the system legible — and what makes designing the human↔AI division of labour an actual, demonstrable skill.

How it works · three ways the team actually used it

Ask a real question. Get a grounded answer.

The same system answers across the whole product lifecycle — understanding the user, understanding the build, and driving the work. Three of the most common asks:

Illustrative stand-in domain. The real product is under NDA, so every example below uses an invented neutral domain — a consumer habit-building app (helping someone keep a daily reading streak). The machinery is real; the domain is fabricated.
01 · Customer research

Transcripts → a problem brief

  • You ask“Here are six interview transcripts — what’s the real problem?”
  • It doesA skill clusters the recurring pains, pulls the verbatim quote behind each, and checks them against the target user — dropping edge cases.
  • You getA structured problem brief: validated pain, cited evidence, a starting direction and the trade-off it accepts — in seconds, not a day of synthesis.
02 · How the build works

“How does this feature actually work?”

  • You ask“How does the streak counter work — what resets it, and what’s it built on?”
  • It doesReads the tech-spec knowledge base — derived from the live code at HEAD and kept fresh by hooks — instead of you opening the repo.
  • You getA plain-English answer a PM can use: what it does, how it’s configured, the stack behind it, and where it lives — no code-diving.
03 · Driving the workforward-looking

Brief → shipped work, always trackable

  • You ask“Spin up the work for the day-3 brief” — then later, “what’s the status?”
  • It doesA skill scaffolds a project and its tickets in the team’s tracker, scoped straight from the brief; the dev team executes against it.
  • You getLive status on demand — insight to execution to “where is it?” without leaving the conversation.

On example 03: the tracker integration is designed but not yet wired up — it’s where the system was headed: closing the loop from customer insight all the way to execution status. Examples 01 and 02 ran live.

Honest trade-offs

What I’d do differently

23 policies is arguably over-governance for a project this young — for a smaller team I’d start with five and let the rest earn their place. Some primitives were built ahead of need. The right lesson isn’t “build all of this”; it’s “build the observe / judge / execute spine, then let the system tell you what it’s missing.”
